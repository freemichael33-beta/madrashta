Classical Syriac: a short history

Syriac is the literary form of Aramaic that grew up in Edessa and became the written language of Christians across the Middle East and Asia. It is still read in church today and is taught at universities around the world.

Origins in Edessa

Aramaic was the common language of the Near East from the Assyrian and Persian empires onwards. Syriac began as the local Aramaic dialect of Edessa (Urhoy, today Şanlıurfa in south-eastern Turkey), the capital of the small kingdom of Osroene.

The oldest dated Syriac inscription, found near Birecik on the Euphrates, comes from the year 6 AD. Early inscriptions, mosaics and legal documents from the first three centuries show a written standard that later became the shared literary language of a much wider region.

The language of a Christian civilisation

Edessa became one of the first centres of Christianity, and Syriac its written language. The Bible was translated into Syriac between the 2nd and 5th centuries; the standard version, the Peshitta, is still read in church. Tatian's Diatessaron, a single gospel narrative of about 170 AD, also circulated in Syriac.

The 4th century is the classical age of Syriac literature. Aphrahat wrote his Demonstrations in the Persian empire, and Ephrem the Syrian (about 306–373) composed hymns and verse homilies that are still sung. The School of Edessa, and after 489 the School of Nisibis, trained generations of teachers and translators.

Two traditions, three scripts

After the councils of Ephesus (431) and Chalcedon (451), Syriac-speaking Christians divided into an eastern tradition, the Church of the East, and a western one, the Syriac Orthodox Church. The language stayed the same, but pronunciation and script drifted apart.

Three scripts developed from the older Estrangela: Serto in the west and the East Syriac (Madnhaya) script in the east, the one used for Modern Assyrian today. Both write from right to left with 22 consonants; the west marks vowels with signs taken from Greek letters, the east with systems of dots.

From Greek to Arabic, and as far as China

Syriac scholars translated Greek philosophy, medicine and science. Sergius of Reshaina (died 536) translated Galen and Aristotle, and in 9th-century Baghdad Hunayn ibn Ishaq and his circle translated Greek works into Syriac and Arabic. This work carried much of Greek learning into the Islamic world and later to Europe.

Missionaries of the Church of the East took Syriac along the Silk Road to Central Asia, China and India. The Xi'an stele of 781 records their mission in Chinese and Syriac, and the Saint Thomas Christians of Kerala still use Syriac in their liturgy.

Decline and survival

After the Arab conquests, Arabic slowly replaced Syriac in everyday life. A last flowering, the Syriac renaissance of the 11th–13th centuries, ended with the polymath Bar Hebraeus (1226–1286), who wrote on grammar, history, philosophy and medicine.

Syriac remained the liturgical and scholarly language of the Assyrian Church of the East, the Chaldean Catholic, Syriac Orthodox, Syriac Catholic and Maronite churches, and the Syriac churches of India. The spoken Neo-Aramaic languages, Sureth and Turoyo, are close relatives of Classical Syriac rather than direct descendants; they share its script and much of its vocabulary, and Syriac is often called the 'book language' (Kthobonoyo).

Syriac studies today

European scholarship on Syriac began in the 16th century; the first printed Syriac New Testament appeared in Vienna in 1555. Today Syriac is taught at universities in Europe, the Americas, the Middle East, India and Australia, from first-year language courses to doctoral research.

Digital projects such as Syriaca.org and the Beth Mardutho research library make manuscripts and reference works freely available. The table below lists universities and academic institutes where you can study Syriac.
