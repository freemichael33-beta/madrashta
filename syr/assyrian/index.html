Aramaic in the Assyrian Empire

Aramaic was first the language of the Aramaean states of Syria. In the 9th and 8th centuries BC the Neo-Assyrian Empire absorbed these states and resettled many of their people across its lands. The statue of Tell Fekheriye, from about 850 BC, already carries the same text in Akkadian and in Aramaic.

From the reign of Tiglath-Pileser III (745–727 BC), Aramaic was used beside Akkadian in the imperial administration. Palace reliefs show pairs of scribes: one writes Akkadian on a clay tablet, the other Aramaic with a pen on leather or papyrus. When the envoys of Sennacherib came to Jerusalem in 701 BC, the king's officials asked them to speak Aramaic, the language of diplomacy (2 Kings 18:26).

A language that outlived empires

Nineveh fell in 612 BC, but Aramaic stayed. The Neo-Babylonian kings and then the Persian Achaemenid Empire (539–330 BC) used it as their official written language from Egypt to India. Scholars call this standard Imperial Aramaic.

After Alexander the Great, each region developed its own written Aramaic: Nabataean and Palmyrene in the west, the Jewish Aramaic of Palestine and Babylonia, Mandaic in southern Mesopotamia, and Syriac in Edessa. Syriac became the literary language of Christians in Mesopotamia and beyond, and it is still the church language of the Assyrians.

Neo-Aramaic: the spoken languages of today

Arabic replaced Aramaic in most cities after the 7th century, but spoken Aramaic lived on in the mountains and villages. Linguists divide today's Neo-Aramaic into four groups: Western Neo-Aramaic in Maaloula near Damascus; Central Neo-Aramaic, mainly Turoyo (Surayt) of Tur Abdin; North-Eastern Neo-Aramaic, to which Sureth belongs; and Neo-Mandaic in Iran.

Modern Assyrian is the largest of these languages. Linguists list it as Assyrian Neo-Aramaic (ISO 639-3: aii); the closely related speech of the Nineveh Plain is listed as Chaldean Neo-Aramaic (cld). Speakers usually call their language Sureth or Swadaya, 'the language of the people'.

The dialects of Sureth

Sureth was spoken in a wide area between the Urmia plain in Iran, the Hakkari mountains in Turkey and the plains of northern Iraq. Every valley and tribe had its own variety, and they differ in pronunciation, vocabulary and some grammar, but speakers understand each other.

The main groups are listed in the table below. In the 20th century, Assyrians who moved to Baghdad, Kirkuk and Habbaniya formed a common Iraqi Koine. Together with the Urmia standard it is the variety most often heard in the diaspora, in music and in the media.

Writing and literature

Sureth is written in the East Syriac (Madnhaya) script, with vowel points above and below the letters. In the 1830s and 1840s American missionaries in Urmia, working with Assyrian scholars, set up a printing press and made the Urmia dialect the basis of a written standard. The first Assyrian newspaper, Zahrire d-Bahra ('Rays of Light'), appeared in Urmia in 1849.

Since then Sureth has had its own poetry, plays, journalism and songs. Many words for church, learning and abstract ideas come from Classical Syriac, while everyday speech also borrowed from Kurdish, Persian, Arabic and Turkish.

Sureth today

Wars and persecution in the 20th and 21st centuries scattered Assyrians across the world. Most speakers now live in Iraq, Iran, Syria, the United States, Australia, Sweden, Germany and other countries, and fewer children learn the language at home. UNESCO counts it among the world's endangered languages.

Schools, church classes, online courses and media are keeping Sureth alive. The directory on this site lists places where you can learn it, and the timeline above shows how the language you are learning connects to almost three thousand years of Aramaic.
